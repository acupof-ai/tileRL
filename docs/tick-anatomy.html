The shape is the whole argument for speculation on this card. Verify checks four candidate tokens for the price of one trunk pass, because a trunk pass is bound by reading 14.43 GB of weights and four rows read the same weights as one. The draft is three sequential passes over a 456M-parameter head — cheap per pass, but they cannot overlap: each position needs the hidden state the previous one produced.

Dense decode is the control, and it is the one that reconciles: 1.7× off its floor, running entirely inside a captured CUDA graph. Verify runs the same kernels over 4 rows and is 3.1× off. Draft runs outside the graph, but that turned out not to be what costs it: see the correction below.

Is the implementation right?

Numerically, yes — and the pieces that were wrong are documented. The head loads with the correct RMSNorm convention (a Qwen NextN head is zero-centred, y = x·(1+w); a DSpark head is plain w·x, and folding the wrong one produced logits anti-correlated with the trunk, argmax ranked 248191/248320). fc consumes concat(norm_embed, norm_hidden) in that order; the other order looks like a head that simply does not predict. Acceptance is 3.34 tokens per forward, and drafted-token rank inside the trunk's own ordering is top-1 96.8%.

Structurally, the tick is 88% GPU-bound, so there is little for the host to give back. _draft_step does run outside the captured graph (engine.py:836), but per-kernel attribution puts 58.5 ms of GPU-busy in a 66.46 ms tick — capturing every remaining launch caps at 1.14×, and 71% of that GPU time is a single kernel (linear_fp4_gemv_sm70_m, 332 launches × 125 µs = 41.49 ms) which a graph replays at the same speed. Depths 2 and 3 both dispatch rung 4, so verify is identical between them and their difference is exactly one draft forward: 5.53 ms. Feeding that back through depths 1 and 4 reproduces the verify staircase (36.58 / 49.87 / 49.87 / 68.46, rung ratios 1.363 and 1.373), which is what turns one subtraction into a measurement.

Correction to an earlier claim

Three of my own write-ups say the sync fix took a draft forward from 15.13 ms to 5.53 ms. That attribution is wrong. Both depth sweeps — before and after the fix — produced the same tick times to within 0.5% (42.36/61.29/66.76/90.96 then 42.11/60.93/66.46/90.58). Had three forwards each dropped 9.6 ms, the depth-3 tick would have fallen to ~37 ms. It did not. 15.13 was never a draft cost: it was the slope of a line fitted through a staircase, which charges verify's growth to the draft. The sync fix's own benefit was never measured separately, and the commit message, CHANGELOG and wins entry all need correcting.

What the two gaps mean

They turn out to be the same problem, and it is not speculation's. Both the draft and verify spend their time in one kernel at 125 µs per launch. Verify's 3.1× is the schedule question that caps prefill: the sm70 fp4 GEMV reaches 83% of bandwidth peak at M=1 and roughly a quarter of packed-f16 FLOP peak at M=32, because X is reloaded from L2 per row per tile. X[32,5120] in f16 is 328 KB — it fits the 6 MB L2 but not the 128 KB L1, so those reads sit on the FMA critical path at ~200 cycles.

Two candidate fixes for that have already been measured and rejected: raising n_partition is flat from 4 to 16 and 28% worse at 32, and splitting the kernel's 8-deep FMA accumulator chain is 1.00–1.01× at every M. Staging X in shared memory is the one remaining candidate with a mechanism behind it rather than an analogy — it removes the loads instead of reordering the math, which is precisely what those two measurements showed does not help.

One kernel is 71% of the tick, at a per-launch cost that barely moves with M. That is the whole optimisation surface, and it is a schedule question, not a launch-overhead one. The 332 launches are ~305 for the trunk's verify plus ~9 per draft forward × 3.

Second-largest is not model math: 252 launches of torch’s index_elementwise, the chain bookkeeping in _draft_step and _verify. Smaller than the GEMV work but larger than the GDN scan, and removable without restructuring anything.

The profiler that produced this table reads 121.5 ms/tick where the real tick is 66.46 — torch.profiler serialises launches, so its wall clock and its “52% host” line are instrument artifacts and are not used here. A sum of kernel durations survives that distortion; a wall-clock ratio does not.
